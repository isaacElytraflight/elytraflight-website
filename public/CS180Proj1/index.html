Images of the Russian Empire: Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii photo collection

Long before the first true color photograph, a man named Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii was thinking far ahead of his time. In the early 1900's, not long after the first black and white photographs were being taken, Gorskii toured around Tsarist Russia, taking pictures of everything he found relevant. The catch was that, instead of regular black and white photos, he used three separate color filters: one for red, one for green, and one for blue. This resulted in a stack of images, each of which were still black and white; but, Gorskii reasoned, eventually scientists would be able to overlay the images and display his photos in their original, vibrant color.

Gorskii was right. And that's exactly what this project is all about--taking photos that are over a century old and bringing them back to life, with the help of computational photography techniques.

Measuring similarity with NCC

So then, we must ask the question: "How do we determine if two images overlap well, so that the brute force algorithm knows how to identify a match?" There are several ways of doing this, but I implemented a form of NCC (Normalized Cross-Correlation) comparison. The idea is that we normalize the two images we want to compare, so that their average is "0". We then stretch them out into one, massive 1D vector. What this lets us do is take the dot product of the two vectors, to get a score for how aligned the image vectors are! If the image vectors are off by a certain offset, their pixel values will not line up, and likely cancel each other out; whereas an aligned image has a very high dot product, as each peak in one image amplifies the identical peak in the other image, etc.

This is how NCC works. In my specific version, I divide by the dot product of img2 with itself; this is to yield a metric I call "cosine similarity" in my code. It's called this because the cosine of the angle between two vectors in N dimensions is their dot product divided by the product of the magnitude of both vectors. I got a bit confused while coding, because dividing the magnitude of img2 squared is NOT technically cosine similarity--rather, it's the multiplier for img1 if we wanted to project it onto the span of img2! That's okay for this situation, though, because as long as im1 and im2 are just shifted versions of each other, they have the same Euclidean magnitude, meaning it is functionally equal to cosine similarity. It's also important to note that, since im1 is shifting and im2 is notably not shifting, dividing by the square magntidue of im2 has no effect on the final result compared to comparing the dot product directly, as true NCC does; I just did it this way so I can keep the NCC score between 0 and 1 for easy readability during the debugging process. It also means that smaller or bigger images stay on the same scale!

Here is the scoring function I used:

def cosineScore(im1, im2):
    # Effectively NCC, with img2 size normalization (easier to debug).
    img1 = im1[:].copy()
    img2 = im2[:].copy()
    img1 = img1.reshape(-1)
    img2 = img2.reshape(-1)
    img1 -= np.mean(img1)
    img2 -= np.mean(img2)
    img1 /= np.std(img1)
    img2 /= np.std(img2)
    return np.dot(img1, img2) / np.dot(img2, img2)

Image pyramids

Now, we get to the much more difficult part, image pyramid alignment. The essential idea is shown in the GIF below: make image alignment O(log(size)) rather than O(size) by aligning successively scaled-down versions of the image.

The way I implemented this is as follows:

def align:

  1. If the image is smaller than 200 pixels, use the naive brute force approach.
  2. Otherwise, recursively call align on a version of the image that is shrunk down by a particular "cut Rate" on both axes. I choose 2 for the cut rate.
  3. Take the "x" and "y" values that the smaller version of myself outputted as the shift; then multiply those "x" and "y" values by the cut Rate, shift the image over by that rough value. At this point, the image should again be roughly "centered" about (0, 0) again, at least on a smaller scale.
  4. Call bruteforce align for fine alignment on this roughly-aligned version of the image.
  5. Add up the rough and fine adjustments, shift the picture accordingly, and return the final "x" and "y" along with the image, in case it's needed up stream for a larger version of myself

Bells and Whistles

One bell and whistle I wanted to implement was automatic white balancing. It's not super sophisticated, but I went off of the principle that, in a balanced picture, the average value for the red channel, the green channel, and the blue channel, should be roughly identical. So I found the mean of each, and normalized each color channel to the overall mean value of the entire picture. 

means = np.mean(im_out, (0, 1))
OVERALL = np.mean(means)

multipliers = OVERALL/means

im_out[:, :]*=multipliers
means = np.mean(im_out, (0, 1))
#print(means)
im_out = np.clip(im_out, 0, 1)
